Analog Cathodoluminescence

CRT: Cathode Ray Tube Mechanics

Invented by Karl Ferdinand Braun in 1897, the CRT relies on an electron gun operating inside a sealed high-vacuum glass envelope. A heated cathode emits electrons through thermionic emission, which are focused and accelerated by anodes at 20,000–30,000 Volts toward the front faceplate.

Electromagnetic Deflection Yoke: Copper coils create orthogonal magnetic fields that sweep the electron beam horizontally (15kHz–130kHz) and vertically in a raster pattern line by line.
Shadow Mask & Phosphor Triads: A perforated metal sheet precisely aligns three separate beams (Red, Green, Blue) to strike corresponding phosphor dots on the glass faceplate, releasing visible light upon kinetic impact.
Zero Input Lag & Multi-Sync: Because there is no frame buffer, scalar, or digital matrix conversion, video signals are directly transformed into deflection voltages with practically 0ms latency.

Still cherished in Retro Esports (Melee, NES, 240p Arcade)
Polarized Light Valve

LCD: Twisted Molecular Light Valves

Unlike CRTs and OLEDs, a Liquid Crystal Display (LCD) is strictly transmissive and non-emissive. Liquid crystals themselves emit zero light; they act as molecular shutters that twist and guide light between two crossed polarizing filters at 90° angles.

TN Panel Twisted Nematic. Fast response (1ms), poor viewing angles & washed color gamut.
IPS Panel In-Plane Switching. Superior 178° viewing angles and color fidelity. Low native contrast (~1000:1).
VA Panel Vertical Alignment. Higher native contrast (3000:1–5000:1), but notorious for dark level smearing.
Voltage Control Mechanism: In the relaxed state, nematic liquid crystals twist light 90°, allowing it through the perpendicular front polarizer (pixel ON). When voltage is applied via the TFT electrode, molecules align parallel to the electric field, preventing light rotation so the front polarizer blocks the light (pixel OFF).

Backlight Revolution & Micro-LED

LED Displays: Clarifying the Great Industry Myth

Consumer television marketing calls products "LED TVs", yet mechanically they are still liquid crystal panels. The only distinction from vintage LCDs is the light source: cold fluorescent tubes (CCFL) were replaced by solid-state Light Emitting Diodes (LEDs).

Edge-Lit LED Slim / Budget

LED strips placed solely along outer frame edges. Light is distributed via acrylic guide plates. Suffers from severe edge vignetting and poor contrast.

Full-Array Local Dimming (FALD) & Mini-LED High-End HDR

Tens of thousands of miniaturized LEDs placed directly behind the LCD in hundreds or thousands of independently controlled zones. Reaches searing 2,000 to 4,000 nits, but produces noticeable "blooming / halo" around bright objects on dark backgrounds.

True Direct Micro-LED (The Holy Grail) Next-Gen

Inorganic microscopic LEDs acting as individual self-emissive subpixels without an LCD layer. Combines the infinite black of OLED with 5000+ nits brightness and zero burn-in risk. Currently cost-prohibitive for mass consumer adoption.

Self-Emissive Electroluminescence

OLED: Per-Pixel Self-Emissive Mastery

OLED replaces backlights, diffusers, and liquid crystal light valves completely. Each individual subpixel consists of thin films of organic (carbon-based) semiconductor compounds that emit light directly when an electric current passes between an anode and cathode.

True Infinite Contrast (0.0000 nits)

When a pixel needs to represent black, it is completely switched off. Contrast ratio is mathematically undefined / infinite (X / 0 = ∞). Absolute zero backlight bleed or halo effect.

Sub-Millisecond Response Time (~0.03ms)

Unlike liquid crystals that require physical mechanical re-orientation inside viscous fluid, organic diode electroluminescence changes state near the speed of electron drift, virtually eliminating transition ghosting.

WOLED vs QD-OLED Architecture

LG WOLED: White OLED emitter + RGBW color filters. Samsung QD-OLED: Pure Blue OLED emitter passing through printed Quantum Dot nanocrystals for 90%+ Rec.2020 color volume.

Caveat: Blue organic diode lifetime degradation & ABL (Auto Brightness Limiter)

Optical Dynamic Range

Contrast & Black Level Depth

Human visual perception of image depth, sharpness, and "pop" is overwhelmingly dictated by dynamic range and black floor. If blacks are milky or gray, shadows collapse and colors look desaturated.

LCD (CCFL / Edge-LED) ~0.15 - 0.30 nits

Because the backlight never turns off, residual light always leaks around liquid crystal borders. Night scenes appear washed out dark gray.

Mini-LED FALD ~0.001 - 0.05 nits

Zones shut off for large dark regions, but a tiny bright star forces an entire 2cm backlight zone to illuminate, creating the infamous "halo blooming" artifact.

OLED & QD-OLED 0.0000 nits (True Zero)

A single white pixel can shine at 1,000 nits immediately adjacent to a pitch-black pixel emitting exactly zero photons. Per-pixel local dimming with 8,294,400 zones on 4K.

Motion Blur & Eye-Tracking Physics

Motion Clarity: Sample-and-Hold vs Impulse

Why did an old 60Hz CRT display look razor-sharp during fast motion, while early 60Hz LCDs smeared like petroleum jelly? The secret lies in Impulsive Scanning vs Sample-and-Hold persistence.

CRT Impulsive Scan (Strobe Effect)

The electron beam strikes each phosphor for mere microseconds; the phosphor rapidly glows and decays before the eye tracks to the next position. Zero retinal persistence blur!

LCD Sample-and-Hold + Liquid Crystal Sluggishness

The panel illuminates continuously, holding the frame for the full 16.6ms (at 60Hz). As human retinas track moving objects, continuous illumination paints the previous frame across moving retina sensors, causing severe perceived blur.

OLED: Instantaneous Response, but Still Sample-and-Hold

OLED transitions in an astonishing 0.03ms (eliminating ghosting trails), but because pixels stay illuminated between frames, high refresh rates (240Hz, 360Hz, 480Hz) or BFI (Black Frame Insertion) are required to match CRT clarity.

Section 1

The Fundamental Physics of Visual Display

Human visual perception relies on photons striking photoreceptors (rods and cones) across the retina. Display technology is essentially an applied optoelectronic apparatus designed to synthesize spatial light patterns with controlled wavelengths (color), flux densities (luminance), and update temporalities (refresh rates).

Fundamentally, all four display technologies fall into two broad thermodynamic architectures:

Emissive Displays (CRT, OLED, Micro-LED)

Every sub-element generates its own light directly. There is no central lamp. When an emissive subpixel is unpowered, it generates exactly zero photons, allowing absolute true black, infinite contrast, and ultra-wide viewing angles without color shift.

Transmissive Displays (LCD, CCFL, Edge-LED, Mini-LED)

The panel is merely an optical shutter (light valve). A blanket backlight illuminates behind the entire screen continuously; liquid crystals rotate light polarization to either allow photons through or attempt to absorb them. Because light valves cannot block 100% of light, contrast is finite and backlight bleed is physically inevitable.

Section 2

Cathode Ray Tube (CRT): The Ballistic Electron Legacy

The CRT remained the undisputed standard of visual reproduction for over 70 years. Inside an evacuated glass envelope, a tungsten heater warms an oxide-coated cathode to ~1000K, expelling free electrons into the vacuum via Richardson's Law of thermionic emission.

Aperture Grille vs Shadow Mask

Traditional CRT monitors used perforated shadow masks with circular holes. Sony revolutionized this with the Trinitron Aperture Grille, utilizing vertical tensioned wires that allowed higher electron throughput, superior horizontal brightness, and sharper vertical focus.

Multi-Sync Flexibility

Modern LCDs have a rigid native pixel matrix (e.g. 1920x1080). Running at non-native resolutions results in fuzzy interpolation blur. CRTs have no native fixed pixels horizontally; the continuous electron beam naturally renders 240p, 480p, 720p, or 1024x768 with pixel-perfect edge clarity.

Why CRT Fell from Grace

Physics imposed brutal limitations: glass required heavy lead shielding to absorb hazardous X-rays generated by 30kV electrons; weight scaled with the cube of diagonal size (a 36" CRT weighed 100+ kg); and high magnetic sensitivity caused geomagnetic color discoloration.

Section 4

LED, Mini-LED & Quantum Dot (QLED) Systems

The evolution of the LED monitor is essentially an evolution in backlight spatial granularity and spectral purity. Standard white LEDs emit a broad blue spike with yellowish phosphor coating. Quantum Dots revolutionized this by taking pure blue photons from GaN LEDs and exciting semiconductor nanocrystals (Cadmium Selenide or Indium Phosphide) whose physical size (2-6 nanometers) quantum-mechanically dictates the exact emitted wavelength:

Small Dot (~2nm diameter): Emits high-energy short wavelengths (vibrant, saturated green at ~530nm).
Large Dot (~6nm diameter): Emits lower energy longer wavelengths (deep pure spectral red at ~630nm).

Zone Granularity & The Halo Limit

A 65-inch 4K screen possesses 8,294,400 pixels. If a Mini-LED TV features 1,152 local dimming zones, each zone still controls approximately 7,200 individual pixels. Therefore, fine high-contrast elements (white subtitles, stars against space, or UI cursors) inevitably illuminate unwanted black pixels surrounding them, generating noticeable light bloom.

Section 5

OLED: Electroluminescence & Organic Solid-State Physics

Discovered by Ching W. Tang and Steven Van Slyke at Eastman Kodak in 1987, organic electroluminescence occurs when current is injected across conjugate carbon polymers. Holes from the Indium Tin Oxide (ITO) anode and electrons from the metallic cathode migrate into the emissive layer, forming neutral bound electron-hole pairs called excitons. As excitons transition to lower energy ground states, the binding energy is released directly as photons.

The Singlet vs Triplet Quantum Problem

Quantum mechanics dictates that electrical excitation yields 25% singlet excitons (fluorescent) and 75% triplet excitons (phosphorescent). Early OLEDs lost 75% of energy as non-radiative heat! Modern displays employ organometallic iridium/platinum phosphorescent dopants (PHOLED) or TADF (Thermally Activated Delayed Fluorescence) to capture nearly 100% internal quantum efficiency.

Burn-in Mechanisms & Mitigations

Blue organic emitters possess wider bandgaps (~3.1 eV), subjecting molecular bonds to higher stress and accelerated photo-oxidation. Over thousands of hours with static UI elements (news tickers, taskbars), differential subpixel degradation causes permanent retention. Manufacturers mitigate this with pixel shifting, logo luminance limiters, and autonomous background pixel compensation cycles.

Section 9

Technical Terminology Glossary

Nit (cd/m²): The standard SI unit of luminance. One nit equals one candela per square meter of luminous intensity.
ABL (Auto Brightness Limiter): A firmware safety circuit in OLEDs that dims full-field bright scenes to protect organic diodes from thermal overload.
BFI (Black Frame Insertion): Strobing black frames between active video frames to clear retinal persistence and simulate CRT-like motion clarity.
Rec. 2020 / BT.2020: An ultra-wide color space defining 75.8% of all visible colors discernable by the human eye; current target for QD-OLED.
Birefringence: The optical property of liquid crystals having a refractive index that depends on the polarization and propagation direction of light.
Raster Scanning: The pattern of sweeping an electron beam across a display horizontally from left to right, then returning diagonally during blanking.
